## Introduction

Italy came from behind to defeat France 3–1 at San Siro. Michael Olise gave France an early lead, but Pio Esposito equalised before half-time. Davide Frattesi and a second Esposito goal then completed the comeback.

The result was notable for the contrast between possession and effectiveness. France controlled more of the ball, but Italy converted their opportunities and made the decisive moments count.

## France strike first

France took the lead in the 10th minute through Michael Olise.

Italy responded in the 26th minute when Pio Esposito scored the equaliser. The goal prevented France from building on their early advantage and brought the teams level at half-time.

The first-half statistics showed France with 59% possession compared with Italy's 41%. France also recorded more attempts, but Italy remained in the contest and created opportunities of their own.

## Italy find greater attacking impact after the break

Italy made two substitutions in the 62nd minute, introducing Antonio Vergara and Federico Dimarco.

Davide Frattesi scored one minute later to put Italy ahead.

The timing was significant, although it would be inaccurate to attribute the goal solely to the substitutions without examining the complete attacking sequence. What can be established is that Italy made changes and quickly converted their advantage into a lead.

Pio Esposito then scored from the penalty spot in the 75th minute to make it 3–1.

## Possession does not guarantee control

France finished the match with 60% possession and 18 attempts, compared with Italy's 40% possession and 10 attempts.

Yet both sides recorded five attempts on target.

These figures show why possession alone is an incomplete measure of match control. France circulated the ball more frequently and attempted more shots, but Italy were more effective at converting their attacking opportunities into goals.

Italy's ability to remain competitive without dominating possession was an important part of the result.

## The tactical takeaway

Italy's victory was built on defensive resistance and attacking efficiency. They responded to France's early goal, remained level at half-time and took advantage of important moments after the interval.

France had the greater share of possession and attempted more shots, but those advantages did not translate into the same scoreboard impact.

The match offers a useful reminder for tactical analysis: territorial dominance, possession and attacking effectiveness are related, but they are not interchangeable.

## TGO Analyst View

Italy's victory provides a useful example of why possession should not be treated as a standalone indicator of dominance. France had more possession and attempted more shots, but Italy demonstrated greater effectiveness in converting their attacking opportunities.

Italy's ability to remain competitive without dominating the ball was central to the result. Their defensive organisation helped them withstand periods of French possession, while their attacking execution allowed them to change the scoreline decisively.

The second-half substitutions also form an important part of the match narrative. Frattesi scored shortly after Italy introduced Vergara and Dimarco, although the timing alone does not establish a direct causal relationship.

France's greater possession did not translate into sufficient scoreboard impact. Italy, by contrast, managed the decisive moments more effectively.

The broader tactical lesson is that possession, territorial control and attacking efficiency must be evaluated separately before drawing conclusions about which team controlled a match.
